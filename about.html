About

Why I'm the one
building this.

The fair question about a tool like this is who made it, and whether they know what they're talking about. Here's the whole answer, including the parts that don't help me.

I'm Amir Keshavarzi. I'm 22, I live in North Vancouver, and I don't have children. I run a small vehicle-management business here, I'm a student, and I built Mend on evenings and weekends.

So the obvious objection is the right one to start with: what does a 22-year-old with no kids know about losing your temper at your child at bedtime? Directly, nothing. I have never stood outside a four-year-old's door at 9 PM feeling the specific shame this whole product is about. I'm not going to pretend otherwise, because pretending is exactly the thing that would make you right to distrust me.

What I have is narrower and, I think, more useful than a pretend qualification. I grew up watching adults I love lose their tempers and not know how to come back from it — and I watched what the coming-back, when it happened, did. The repair mattered more than the blow-up. That's the one idea underneath everything here, and it's the one thing I'm confident about.

Why build it anyway

Because the thing I keep hearing, from parents who do have kids, is that the moment nobody helps with is the twenty minutes after. Every app aims at the shouting. Nobody built anything for 9:04 PM, when it's over and you'd do anything to make it right and you have no idea what to actually do.

I can't tell a parent how to feel in that moment. But I can build something small and specific and honest that sits there when it happens, instead of another course you'll never finish. Being 22 means I have the time to do the unglamorous version of this properly — the year of interviews and writing and no shortcuts — rather than raising money and rushing it.

Who checks the words

This is the part that matters most, so I'll be exact about where it stands today.

A registered clinical counsellor will review every word Mend says before the app is in anyone's hands. Not the marketing — the actual language a parent reads in a hard moment, and the safety logic that decides when Mend shows resources instead of a breathing exercise. I have not signed that person yet. When I have, her name goes on this page, because a credential you can't check is worth nothing.

I'm also getting the repair-language feature read by someone who works in intimate-partner violence, specifically to find how it could be misused, and a Canadian privacy lawyer is reviewing how data is handled before launch. None of that is done yet. I'd rather tell you it's coming than imply it's finished.

Until those reviews are done, Mend is not therapy, not counselling, and not a substitute for a professional. It says so on every page, and it will keep saying so even after the reviews, because it will still be true.

What I'll do if I get it wrong

A tool that touches parents, children, shame and anger can hurt people if it's careless. I take that seriously enough to say what accountability actually looks like, not just that I care.

  • If Mend ever says something that harms rather than helps, I want to hear it. hello@mend.example reaches me, one person, directly.
  • If what someone logs suggests a child is being hurt, Mend shows real resources rather than a reflection prompt. I would rather lose a customer than get that wrong.
  • If the clinical review comes back and says a feature is unsafe, that feature doesn't ship — even the ones I'm proud of. I've written that rule down for myself in advance so future-me can't argue with it.
  • Your logs are yours. They're never sold, never used to train anything, and you can export or delete them.

That's the honest version. If it makes you more cautious about Mend rather than less, that's a reasonable place to land — and you can watch this page to see whether the promises above turn into names and dates.
